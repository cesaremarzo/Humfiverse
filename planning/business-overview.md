# Music Royalty Stream Tokenization — Concept & Business Overview

*Working draft — v0.1, 26 Aug 2026*

**Diagramma del flusso attori (investitori · artista · società di gestione · esecutori), incl. la governance ibrida della SPV:** https://claude.ai/code/artifact/065fee44-78f2-48a3-a23f-e6fbda486b99

## 1. The idea in one paragraph

Music copyrights (compositions and master recordings) generate recurring royalty income from streaming, mechanical, performance, and sync licensing. That income is normally locked up with labels, publishers, and specialized funds, out of reach for ordinary investors and often illiquid even for the rights holders themselves. This project would tokenize the *right to a share of a specific song's (or catalogue's) royalty income*, issuing on-chain tokens that represent a claim on that cash flow, so the claim can be sold in smaller denominations, transferred, and (subject to regulation) traded on a secondary market.

## 2. Why now

Streaming has made royalty income unusually predictable and data-rich (Spotify, Apple Music, and the collection societies all report streams and payouts on a regular cadence), which makes it a good candidate for tokenization compared to less measurable real-world assets. Several platforms have already proven pieces of this model:

- **Royal** (royal.io, founded 2021 by DJ 3LAU and JD Ross) sells "Limited Digital Assets" — NFT-like tokens tied to tiered shares of a song's streaming royalties — and has run payouts to fans since 2022, with artists including Nas, Diplo, and The Chainsmokers.
- **anotherblock** tokenizes streaming rights on Ethereum and Optimism, letting fans buy fractional shares of songs and earn a cut of streaming revenue.
- **JKBX** took the opposite technical path: SEC-qualified securities (not blockchain tokens) representing fractional royalty income from compositions and masters, sold through a dedicated broker-dealer structure, with per-song offerings (e.g., a Beyoncé composition at ~3.45% estimated yield).

The market has therefore already validated investor appetite for fractional royalty ownership; what differs between projects is the legal wrapper (security vs. "digital collectible") and the settlement rail (blockchain token vs. traditional cap table). That's the design space this project sits in, and it's the first strategic decision to make deliberately rather than by default — see §5.

## 3. Who this is for

- **Retail/fan investors** who want small-denomination, song-level exposure to royalty income rather than buying a whole catalogue or an opaque royalty fund.
- **Independent artists and small catalogue owners** who want liquidity or upfront capital against future royalties without giving up full ownership to a major or a royalty-buyout fund (the Hipgnosis/Royalty Exchange model, but with fractional, faster settlement).
- **Existing royalty funds/catalogue owners** who might tokenize a slice of an existing catalogue to raise capital or provide liquidity to their own LPs.

Pinning down which of these three is the *primary* customer first materially changes the product (marketplace app vs. capital-raising rail vs. secondary-liquidity venue for funds) and the regulatory path, so this is worth a explicit decision early.

## 4. How the money actually flows (plain-language version)

1. A rights holder (artist, publisher, or catalogue fund) enrolls a song or catalogue and its royalty income is verified against collection society, distributor (e.g., a DSP or aggregator), or PRO data.
2. A legal entity (typically an SPV — special-purpose vehicle) is created to hold the actual royalty right; the token represents a claim against that SPV, not the copyright itself. This indirection is what every serious platform in this space uses (JKBX's Jukebx Hits Vol. 1 LLC is the clearest public example) because direct on-chain ownership of a copyright isn't yet a recognized legal structure in most jurisdictions.
3. Tokens representing fractional interests in the SPV's royalty right are sold to investors, either as a security offering (JKBX's path) or as a "digital collectible" with attached revenue rights (Royal's path — a structure that carries more regulatory ambiguity; see the legal doc).
4. Royalty income is collected by the SPV (via a collection account, a PRO, or a royalty administrator) and distributed to token holders periodically — this is the part a smart contract can genuinely automate, once the money has reached an on-chain-controlled account, but it cannot pull the money out of Spotify or a PRO itself. See the technical architecture doc for how the oracle/off-chain-to-on-chain bridge works and why it's the hardest engineering problem here, not the token contract.

## 5. Key decisions this project needs to make deliberately

These are the forks in the road that determine everything downstream (legal structure, tech stack, target user, and go-to-market). I'd suggest we settle these before going deep on any one document:

- **Token = security, or token = "digital collectible" with revenue rights attached?** This is the single biggest determinant of legal cost, timeline, and who can invest. See the legal/regulatory doc for the tradeoffs.
- **Public blockchain (Ethereum/L2) vs. permissioned/consortium chain?** Public chains give composability and secondary-market potential; permissioned chains simplify KYC/AML and reduce regulatory surface area at the cost of network effects.
- **New catalogue originations vs. tokenizing an existing fund's holdings?** Originating deals with individual artists is slower and requires music-industry relationships; partnering with an existing royalty fund to tokenize a slice of an established catalogue is faster to a first pilot.
- **Geography of first launch.** Given your base in Italy/EU, MiCA governs crypto-asset issuance and CASP licensing across the EU as of 2026, but — per the legal doc — a token with the economic characteristics described above is likely to fall *outside* MiCA and *into* MiFID II/national securities law instead, which changes which regulator and which licensing track applies.

## 7. Risk to watch: AI-generated content and royalty-stream integrity

A reasonable concern raised during scoping: will AI-generated music undermine the royalty income this project would tokenize? The current evidence (mid-2026) is more specific than a blanket "AI music becomes unmonetizable," and the nuance matters for how this project should position itself.

- **Platforms are diverging, not converging.** TIDAL began fully demonetizing "100% AI-generated" tracks on 15 July 2026 — no royalties at all, regardless of fraud — while still paying normally on AI-*assisted* tracks with real human creative input. Spotify has taken the opposite stance: it monetizes AI-generated music normally and says its policy is "not about punishing or downranking responsible AI use." So there is no industry consensus yet on whether AI music itself gets cut off — it's a live, platform-by-platform policy question, and a platform can change its stance unilaterally (as TIDAL just did) after a catalogue is already tokenized.
- **The bigger, better-evidenced problem is fraud, not AI authorship per se.** IFPI estimates streaming fraud costs artists roughly $2 billion a year industry-wide. On Deezer specifically, around 85% of streams on AI-generated tracks were bot-driven fraud rather than real listening, and AI-generated uploads reportedly make up close to half of daily new uploads on that platform. Spotify removed over 75 million tracks in the twelve months to September 2025, primarily for spam, stream manipulation, and unauthorized voice clones — not for AI use as such. In other words, the royalty pools this project would tap into are already under real pressure, but from fraud and low-quality mass uploads diluting pro-rata pools, more than from AI music being formally locked out of monetization.
- **What this means for the project, concretely:**
  - Royalty-verification diligence (§2.1 of the technical architecture doc) needs to explicitly check a catalogue's exposure to platform-specific demonetization policy, not just historical payout data — a clean 24-month royalty history doesn't protect against a platform changing the rules going forward.
  - This is arguably a *positioning opportunity*, not just a risk: a platform that only lists diligenced, verified-human (or transparently-disclosed AI-assisted) catalogues, with fraud-pattern screening built into onboarding, is offering investors something the broader streaming royalty pool increasingly can't guarantee on its own — cleaner, more defensible income.
  - Any investor-facing disclosure should flag platform-policy risk (a DSP can change monetization rules after the fact) as a named risk factor, alongside the usual streaming-performance risk.

This doesn't change the core thesis, but it does argue for building catalogue-authenticity and fraud-screening into the onboarding/diligence process from day one rather than treating it as a v2 feature — see the technical architecture doc.

**7.1 A sharper version of this point: production financing as risk transformation, not just cost coverage.** Combine §7 with §8's pre-production model and a genuinely strong idea falls out: financing the human production layer on top of a raw or AI-generated draft — musicians, vocalists, a mix/mastering engineer — doesn't just improve the track creatively, it plausibly moves it along the AI-content spectrum DSPs are starting to police. TIDAL only demonetizes tracks it classifies as "100%"/"substantially" AI-generated; a draft that receives real, documented human creative and technical contribution before release is a materially different asset — both artistically and, likely, in terms of monetization durability — than the same draft released untouched. If the model works this way, the token isn't just funding "finishing the song"; it's funding the conversion of a fragile, policy-exposed asset into a more durable one, which is a genuinely strong pitch to investors.

The honest caveat: as of mid-2026, no platform has published a quantitative threshold for this. TIDAL's own policy defines "100% AI-generated" and reserves the right to later expand demonetization to "substantially AI-generated" content, without specifying what "substantially" means or how much human contribution is enough to exit that bucket; Spotify's DDEX-based disclosure (five categories — AI vocals, instrumentation, composition, post-production, lyrics) is a *disclosure* taxonomy, not a pass/fail human-content test. So this is a real, logical mechanism, not a guaranteed one — the platform should market it as "documented human production materially reduces AI-demonetization exposure," not as "human production guarantees monetization," since the actual bright line is still being written by DSPs in real time and could tighten in ways that are hard to predict today.

This does suggest a concrete product feature, though: since the pre-production financing model (§8) already needs to record production milestones for escrow release, that same record — who played what, which studio, what mixing/mastering was applied, timestamped and stored — is also exactly the evidence a platform would want on hand to (a) generate accurate DDEX disclosure metadata automatically rather than relying on the artist to self-report it correctly, and (b) defend a track's classification if a DSP's policy or detection tooling is ever disputed. Worth building the production-milestone log and the AI-disclosure record as one system, not two — see the technical architecture doc.

## 8. A proposed evolution: pre-production financing with token-holder governance

A refinement worth taking seriously: instead of (or in addition to) tokenizing an *already-monetized* catalogue (§4), artists upload a track in any "raw" state — a demo, an AI-generated draft, an unfinished idea — and minting tokens at upload time raises the money to actually finish it: session musicians, studio time, mixing/mastering, and add-ons like ad campaigns or a music video. Token holders then hold a claim on that specific track's future royalties, and optionally get a vote on production choices (which studio, which session singer) so those choices aren't made opaquely by a single gatekeeper.

There's real precedent that fans will fund music this way: **Corite** already runs a live version of the first half of this — fans back a song pre-release for a royalty *advance*, repaid from streaming revenue over a fixed one-year window, after which sharing stops; Corite takes 5% of the raise plus 5% of future royalties for the distribution service. That validates investor appetite for pre-release funding. It's worth registering, though, that Corite's structure is *time-boxed* (one year, then done) rather than a perpetual royalty share — plausibly a deliberate choice to stay closer to "advance repayment" than "ongoing investment," which is a lighter regulatory lift. This project's model, as described, sounds closer to the heavier version (perpetual or long-dated royalty share in exchange for financing), and that distinction matters a lot for §3 below.

**8.1 This changes the product from "bond-like" to "venture-like."** Tokenizing an established catalogue (§4) is buying a claim on a *known, already-happening* cash flow — closer to a bond. Funding an unfinished or AI-draft track before it's produced, marketed, or released is funding a venture with no track record — most crowdfunded creative projects (records, films, games) never fully recoup for backers, the same way most startup investments don't. Both products can coexist, but they need to be underwritten, priced, and disclosed to investors completely differently. Presenting the second as if it carried the risk profile of the first would be a real problem, both ethically and — per §3 below — legally.

**8.2 The pooled-funds risk has a real precedent, and a real fix.** **PledgeMusic**, a pre-blockchain fan-funding platform, collapsed in 2019 after using new campaigns' money to pay out older campaigns once growth outpaced its financial controls — artists were owed hundreds of thousands of dollars that had evaporated. The direct lesson for this design: never let raised funds sit in a commingled operating account the platform can dip into. A genuine advantage of building this on-chain is that it doesn't have to repeat that mistake — funds raised for a track can sit in a **smart-contract escrow that releases against production milestones** (e.g., a tranche on studio booking confirmation, a tranche on mix delivery, a refund path if the campaign doesn't reach its goal or the artist doesn't deliver), auditable by anyone, rather than trusted to a company's back office. This should be treated as a core design requirement, not a nice-to-have — see the technical architecture doc.

**8.3 Token-holder governance over production decisions is the part to slow down on.** The instinct — democratize choices like "which studio" or "which session singer" so they aren't made through opaque, potentially anticompetitive insider relationships — is a reasonable one. But binding crowd votes on operational/hiring decisions carry two distinct problems worth weighing before committing to this design:

- **Legal exposure.** In November 2024, a US federal court (in litigation over the Lido DAO) ruled that participants who vote in a DAO's governance can be treated as *general partners in a partnership* under California law — meaning **personal, joint-and-several liability** for the venture's obligations, not capped at what they invested. The court's reasoning was that real voting over real decisions, backed by a real treasury, is "the actions of an entity run by people," not autonomous software, regardless of how it's marketed. This is now a live legal risk for *any* project giving token holders binding votes over business decisions, and it argues strongly against literal "token holders vote and the result executes automatically" governance — at least without a legal wrapper (e.g., a Wyoming DAO LLC/DUNA-style entity) that caps participants' liability, or without making the vote *advisory* to a party (the SPV manager or the artist) who retains formal legal and contracting responsibility.
- **Creative/product risk, independent of the law.** Committee-driven creative decisions are a known failure mode — it's part of why Corite, Kickstarter, and most successful fan-funding platforms deliberately do *not* give backers control over creative execution, only over whether to fund at all. Full binding votes on "which singer performs this" risk slower decisions, watered-down creative choices, and could make the platform unattractive to exactly the artists it needs, who may not want to fund their work by giving up creative control to a crowd.

A middle path worth considering: keep the anti-opacity goal, drop the binding-execution mechanism. For example, the artist or platform pre-vets a shortlist of qualified studios/collaborators (so there's no opaque single-party pick), token holders vote on that shortlist in an *advisory* capacity, and the SPV manager formally executes and contracts — preserving transparency and reducing gatekeeping without creating partnership-style liability or design-by-committee output. Vote-weight caps or delegation limits would also be worth building in from the start, so the mechanism doesn't just relocate the "opaque concentrated power" problem it's meant to solve into a handful of large token holders instead.

**Sequencing decision (confirmed):** governance — even in the mitigated, advisory-plus-shortlist-plus-vote-cap form above — is deliberately deferred to a later project phase, once the SPV-manager role and the other safeguards are properly in place, rather than built into the pilot. Phase 0–1 (see technical doc §3) run with no token-holder voting at all; the governance module is a Phase 2+ feature. This keeps the pilot focused on proving the harder, more foundational problem (verified royalty data → escrow → distribution) without also having to get a novel governance/liability structure right on the first try.

**8.4 The heavy-compliance path as a deliberate moat, not just a cost.** Worth stating explicitly: choosing the perpetual/long-dated royalty-share structure (§8, the "heavy" version relative to Corite's time-boxed advance) means committing to full securities-grade compliance — MiFID II/national securities law, potentially AIFMD, KYC/AML, ongoing disclosure — which is genuinely expensive and slow to build correctly. But that cost is also a real barrier to entry: regulatory weight like this is exactly the kind of thing few competitors can or will sustain, which is why in adjacent fintech history the companies that invested early in doing licensing properly (rather than launching fast and unlicensed) tended to end up as the durable, category-defining players once the market matured and the unlicensed competitors got shut down or acquired. That's a real strategic argument for taking the heavy path deliberately, as a moat-building bet — but it's a bet: it requires enough capital and patience to survive the multi-year compliance build *before* revenue scales, and it only pays off if the underlying product-market fit is real. Worth naming this explicitly in the decision at §5/§9 rather than treating compliance purely as a cost line.

## 9. Suggested next steps

1. Decide the token-as-security-vs-collectible question with input from the legal doc — this gates almost everything else.
2. Decide which model comes first: tokenizing an established, already-monetized catalogue (§4, bond-like) or pre-production financing of an unfinished/draft track (§8, venture-like) — they have different risk profiles, different investor pitches, and arguably need different regulatory treatment, and starting with both at once would blur the story for regulators, artists, and investors alike.
3. If pursuing §8, resolve the governance-liability question (advisory vs. binding votes, legal wrapper for the collective) with counsel before writing any voting logic — this is now a well-evidenced legal risk, not a hypothetical one.
4. Scope a minimum technical pilot (see technical architecture doc) that proves the royalty-data-to-payout pipeline — and, if §8 is in scope, the milestone-escrow mechanism — before investing in a public-facing marketplace.
5. Build catalogue-authenticity and platform-policy-risk screening into the onboarding diligence process (§7) from the first pilot, not as a later add-on.
