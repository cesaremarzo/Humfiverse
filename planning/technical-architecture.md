# Music Royalty Stream Tokenization — Technical Architecture

*Working draft — v0.1, 26 Aug 2026*

## 1. The hard problem, stated plainly

The smart contract that mints and transfers tokens is the easy part — it's a well-understood pattern. The hard part is everything around it: proving a royalty right exists and is unencumbered, getting real-world royalty income into a wallet a smart contract can act on, and doing payouts and compliance (KYC, transfer restrictions, tax reporting) correctly. Any architecture proposal that starts with "which chain should we use" before addressing those is designing the wrong 20%. This doc addresses them in that order.

## 2. System components

**2.1 Rights & royalty data verification (off-chain, but foundational)**
Before anything is tokenized, the platform needs to confirm: (a) who legally owns the royalty stream being tokenized, (b) that it isn't already pledged/encumbered elsewhere, and (c) what its historical and likely future income looks like. This means integrating with — or at minimum ingesting statements from — performance rights organizations (PROs, e.g. ASCAP/BMI/SIAE for Italy), mechanical licensing bodies, and distributor/DSP reporting (Spotify for Artists, Apple Music for Artists, or an aggregator like DistroKid/Believe that already aggregates this). There is no reliable public API that hands over "ground truth" royalty data automatically — every platform in this space (Royal, anotherblock, JKBX) does this verification largely as a manual/contractual diligence process before listing a song, not as a live feed. Plan for this to be a human-in-the-loop onboarding process, at least in v1.

A fourth check belongs alongside those three from day one: **exposure to platform AI-content policy.** As of mid-2026, DSPs are not aligned — TIDAL demonetizes fully AI-generated tracks outright (since 15 July 2026) while Spotify still pays on them normally, focusing enforcement on fraud/bot streams instead (which IFPI estimates cost artists ~$2B/year industry-wide, and which made up roughly 85% of AI-track streams on Deezer). Because a DSP can change this policy unilaterally after a catalogue is already tokenized, onboarding diligence should record, per catalogue: whether any AI tooling was used in the recording and to what degree, and each target DSP's current AI-monetization stance — and the platform should re-check that periodically, not just once at listing. Building this screening in from the pilot, rather than retrofitting it later, is also a plausible differentiator: a diligenced, fraud-screened, authenticity-labeled catalogue is a cleaner asset than the average royalty pool right now.

Note that no DSP has published a bright-line, quantitative threshold for "how much human contribution exempts a track from AI-demonetization" — TIDAL's policy defines only "100%"/"substantially" AI-generated without specifying what "substantially" means, and Spotify's DDEX categories (AI vocals, instrumentation, composition, post-production, lyrics) are a disclosure taxonomy, not a pass/fail test. So this check should be re-run periodically per catalogue, not treated as a one-time pass/fail gate at onboarding — see §2.7 for how the production-financing variant turns this from a pure risk into a documented, evidenced asset.

**2.2 Legal wrapper: the SPV**
A special-purpose vehicle (SPV — e.g., an Italian *società a responsabilità limitata* set up per catalogue, or per song/bundle) holds the actual royalty right, assigned or licensed from the rights holder under a contract. The token represents a claim on the SPV (equity, a revenue-participation note, or a security token depending on the legal path chosen — see legal doc), never the copyright directly. This is the standard structure because "the copyright itself lives on a blockchain" isn't a recognized legal concept in essentially any jurisdiction today — the token is a claim wrapper around a traditional legal object.

**2.3 The royalty collection account ("la cassa"), the transparency covenant, and the oracle problem**
Royalty income is paid by DSPs/PROs into a traditional bank or collection account controlled by the SPV or a royalty administrator (a specialized service like Sentric, Songtrust, or a custom-built collection agreement) — it does not arrive on-chain natively. This account (call it "la cassa") is the single piece of off-chain infrastructure the whole product depends on, and it is identical whether the track is already live on DSPs or is about to be released once the token sale funds production — which is a useful simplification: **the on-chain side of this product is one architecture, not two.** The rights holder should contractually and technically commit, at the moment the token is created (whether that's at catalogue tokenization or at track upload for the pre-production model), to keep that account's statements public/auditable to token holders — a transparency covenant, not just a payment mechanism. In practice this means the royalty administrator publishes periodic statements (or the platform ingests and republishes them) that any token holder can check against what the distribution contract actually paid out.

To distribute what's in the cassa to token holders, the platform needs a bridge from that off-chain cash position to an on-chain distribution event. In practice this means:
- A royalty administrator or the platform's own finance operations confirms receipt of a royalty payment for a given period.
- That confirmation is submitted on-chain, either by a trusted/multisig-controlled oracle role initially, or later by a decentralized oracle network (e.g. Chainlink Proof of Reserve-style attestation) once volume justifies the added complexity and cost.
- On-chain confirmation triggers a **distribution contract** (deliberately not called a "liquidity pool" — see note below) to pull stablecoin (or fiat via an off-ramp) proportionally to token holders, using a standard "dividend/distribution" pattern (pull-based claims rather than a push to every wallet, to avoid gas-cost and failed-transfer issues at scale).

*Terminology note:* what's needed here is a **revenue-distribution vault**, not a DeFi-style "liquidity pool" (an AMM that matches buyers and sellers and prices an asset via a bonding curve). A literal liquidity pool would introduce price-discovery and impermanent-loss dynamics that have nothing to do with paying out royalty shares, and — more importantly — if it lets holders swap/trade their claims against it, it starts to look like operating a trading venue on top of the SPV structure, which is a separate regulated activity (a trading platform/MTF-equivalent under MiCA/MiFID) layered on top of everything already in the legal doc. Keep the distribution mechanism (accumulate the cassa's confirmed income, let holders claim their pro-rata share) and any future secondary-trading mechanism (§3, Phase 3) as two clearly separate systems, decided separately, rather than defaulting to "liquidity pool" terminology that conflates them.

This oracle step is a trust point: v1 will almost certainly be **centralized/attested** (the platform or a named administrator signs off), not trustless. That's an honest tradeoff to state up front rather than promise more decentralization than is achievable at launch — being transparent about it is also a better regulatory posture than overstating "trustless" claims.

**2.4 Token layer**
- **Chain**: an EVM-compatible chain is the pragmatic default given tooling maturity and precedent (anotherblock uses Ethereum + Optimism). A low-fee L2 (Optimism, Base, Polygon) is the right choice for the actual token/distribution contracts, since royalty payouts can be frequent and small-value; mainnet Ethereum can be used for higher-value anchoring/settlement events if needed. A permissioned chain is worth considering only if the compliance path requires transfer restrictions strict enough that a permissioned ledger meaningfully simplifies enforcement — otherwise it sacrifices liquidity/composability for little benefit.
- **Token standard**: this depends on the legal path (see legal doc). If treated as a security, an ERC-1400/ERC-3643-style permissioned security-token standard (whitelisted transfers, on-chain compliance hooks) is appropriate. If treated as a non-security digital collectible with attached revenue rights (Royal's path), a simpler ERC-1155 (semi-fungible, good for tiered ownership levels) or ERC-20-per-catalogue token can work, but this doc does not take a position on which legal path is right — that's a legal/business decision, not a technical one.
- **Distribution contract**: a claims-based payout contract (holders call `claim()` to pull their share of the latest distribution) is simpler and cheaper than push-based transfers, and is the pattern used by most token dividend/staking-reward systems today.

**2.5 Compliance layer**
KYC/AML on investor onboarding (via a provider like Sumsub or Persona), transfer restrictions enforced either on-chain (whitelist-gated transfers) or off-chain (a permissioned marketplace that only allows verified wallets to trade), and tax reporting (royalty income is taxable income for holders — the platform will likely need to issue equivalent-of-1099/CU-style statements depending on jurisdiction).

**2.6 Front end / marketplace**
A standard web app for browsing songs/catalogues, buying fractional tokens (fiat on-ramp needed — most retail users won't arrive with crypto already), viewing royalty statements, and claiming distributions. This is the least novel part of the stack and can be built with conventional tooling once the above is settled.

Worth a manual pass in a real browser before treating this as fully proven.

**2.7 Pre-production financing variant: milestone escrow + governance module**
If the platform supports funding *unfinished* tracks (see concept doc §8) rather than only tokenizing already-monetized catalogues, two additional components are needed, and they should be designed together since one bounds the other:

- **Milestone-gated escrow.** Funds raised for a track should sit in a smart contract, not a commingled operating account — this is a direct, checkable answer to the failure mode that sank PledgeMusic (a pre-blockchain platform that collapsed in 2019 after using new campaigns' money to cover old ones). A simple version: define 3–4 milestones per campaign (e.g., funding goal reached → studio/collaborators booked → mix/master delivered → release confirmed on DSPs), release a pre-agreed tranche of the escrowed funds at each on confirmation from the SPV manager or an appointed administrator, and build in an automatic refund path if the funding goal isn't met by a deadline or the artist doesn't deliver. **The refund is net of milestones already completed, not a full clawback**: `refundable = total_contributed − Σ(tranches already released for confirmed milestones)`. If, say, the studio-booking and mix-delivery milestones were met and paid out before the campaign stalled before "release confirmed," contributors are refunded pro-rata only the unreleased remainder — the artist keeps what was legitimately spent reaching the milestones actually delivered. This needs a clear dispute path too: what happens if a tranche was drawn but the milestone wasn't genuinely met is a job for the SPV manager/administrator (see legal doc) to adjudicate, not something the smart contract can determine on its own. This escrow design is a genuinely stronger guarantee than a traditional crowdfunding platform can offer, and worth stating plainly in investor-facing material as a differentiator.
- **Governance module — deferred to a later phase, and advisory-first when it ships.** Per the concept and legal docs, binding on-chain votes that directly execute business decisions (hiring a studio, choosing a performer) carry real, current legal exposure (the Lido DAO partnership-liability precedent) and a real product risk (design-by-committee outcomes). This is confirmed as a Phase 2+ feature (§3), not part of the pilot — Phase 0–1 ship with no token-holder voting at all, so the harder, more foundational problem (verified royalty data → escrow → distribution) gets proven first without also debugging a novel governance/liability structure. When it does ship, the lower-risk version: token holders vote on a *shortlist* the artist/platform has already vetted (so there's no single opaque gatekeeper, but also no unbounded crowd decision), the vote result is advisory, and the SPV manager formally executes and signs contracts — with the vote and its outcome recorded on-chain for transparency either way. A vote-weight cap per wallet (or quadratic weighting) is a hard requirement at launch, not a later hardening pass, so the mechanism doesn't just recreate concentrated, opaque control at the level of a few large token holders instead of solving it.
- **Production-milestone log doubles as the AI-disclosure record.** Each milestone confirmation above (studio booked, which musicians/vocalist performed, what mixing/mastering was applied, by whom, when) should be captured as structured, timestamped data, not just a payment trigger. That same record is exactly what's needed to (a) auto-generate accurate DDEX AI-disclosure metadata for DSP submission instead of relying on the artist to self-report it correctly, and (b) evidence how much human creative/technical contribution a track received if a DSP's classification is ever disputed — directly supporting the risk-transformation argument in concept doc §7.1 (documented human production reduces, but per current DSP policy does not guarantee against, AI-demonetization exposure). Building one system that serves the escrow, the disclosure metadata, and the provenance record is simpler and more defensible than three separate ones.

**2.8 Authorship/anti-plagiarism registry (proposed, 28 Aug 2026) — an extension of §2.7's log, not a separate product.** Idea raised: build a public registry of uploaded tracks usable as evidence in artist-vs-artist plagiarism disputes. Two things worth being precise about before building it:
- **It does not touch the SIAE/CMO friction described in the legal doc (§4, §7.4).** Royalty collection (a regulated collective-rights-management activity) and proof-of-authorship timestamping (an unregulated evidentiary service — comparable to SIAE's own "deposito opere inedite" or the existing Italian service Patamu) are legally distinct functions. This registry doesn't reduce the work needed to resolve royalty-assignment terms with whichever society administers a given catalogue.
- **Evidentiary strength depends on the timestamp mechanism, not just having a database.** Italian copyright arises automatically at creation (no registration needed); what a plagiarism dispute actually turns on is *proving priority date*. A plain internal DB row with a timestamp is weak evidence (self-certified, contestable). An eIDAS-qualified electronic timestamp (Regulation (EU) 910/2014), issued by a Qualified Trust Service Provider, carries an EU-wide legal presumption of accuracy for date and data integrity — materially stronger than a bare blockchain anchor alone, though anchoring on top is a reasonable tamper-evidence complement. Neither a timestamp nor a blockchain anchor *proves* original authorship on its own (someone could timestamp a plagiarized work too) — it's supporting evidence a court weighs, not a verdict.
- **Build it as an extension of the existing per-track record**, not a new system: at upload/onboarding, hash the submitted audio/lyrics/stems and timestamp that hash using the same structured log described in §2.7 (ideally via an eIDAS-qualified timestamp service), rather than standing up an independent registry product. This keeps one provenance record serving escrow milestones, AI-disclosure metadata, and authorship evidence, consistent with §2.7's "one system, not three" principle.

**2.9 Projected yield — now computed, not hardcoded (28 Aug 2026).** The "X% proj. yield" figure shown on catalogue listings previously lived as a static number in the mock data, disconnected from the royalty history chart shown right next to it. It's now derived: `yield% = (royalty paid out over the trailing 12 reported months ÷ (tokenPrice × tokensTotal)) × 100` — implemented as `computeYieldBreakdown()`/`computeProjectedYield()` in `docs/index.html`, with a matching info popover on the asset detail page showing the formula, the actual numbers behind the current figure, and an explicit disclaimer that this is a trailing/historical figure, not a promised or guaranteed return. Fixing this exposed that the mock royalty-history generator's base amounts had never been calibrated against `tokenPrice × tokensTotal` — computed yields came out at 37–71% before the seed data's `buildRoyaltyHistory()` base parameters were rescaled to reproduce plausible single-digit yields. A real implementation would compute this the same way from real royalty-administrator statements, not change the underlying principle.

**2.10 First real on-chain code — testnet demo token contract (28 Aug 2026).** `contracts/` is a Hardhat project with `HumfiverseCatalogueToken.sol`, an ERC-1155 contract (OpenZeppelin v5) with one token id per catalogue-kind demo track (`scripts/catalogues.js` — midnight-static, ember-choir, paper-cranes, copper-radio; the two preproduction/milestone-escrow assets are intentionally excluded, since those need the separate escrow mechanism in §2.7, not simple issuance). `mintCatalogue(tokenId, slug, supply)` mints the catalogue's full `tokensTotal` into the contract's own balance — the "platform pool" — using OpenZeppelin's `ERC1155Holder` so the contract can safely hold its own tokens (plain `_mint` to `address(this)` reverts without it — a real bug hit and fixed while building this, not a hypothetical). `releaseFromPool(to, tokenId, amount)` is the only way tokens leave the pool, owner-gated, tracking `releasedOf` against `totalSupplyOf` so it can never over-release. No payment/purchase logic, no KYC/transfer-restriction enforcement — this is purely the issuance-and-pool mechanism, not a compliant offering (§2.4's ERC-3643-style permissioned standard is still what a real security-path launch would need). 8 Hardhat tests cover minting, double-mint protection, owner-gating, pool release/over-release, and multi-catalogue independence — all passing, plus a full deploy→mint dry run against a local Hardhat network before any testnet involvement. Configured for Base Sepolia (consistent with the "low-fee L2" pick in §2.4).

Setting that up on Render puts a real (if low-value, testnet-only) private key into a third-party host's environment variables; this needs the user's explicit go-ahead, not something to do unilaterally.

**2.11 Secondary-market fee infrastructure — HumfiverseMarketplace.sol (29 Aug 2026).** Business rule requested: every platform-mediated token sale *other than* a first purchase retains a 1% platform fee, taken in tokens rather than sale proceeds. Rather than adding an "is this a first purchase?" check somewhere, the fee falls out of keeping two contracts strictly separate:
- `HumfiverseCatalogueToken.releaseFromPool` (§2.10) remains the *only* path for a first purchase — platform pool → buyer, owner-gated, no fee, unchanged.
- `HumfiverseMarketplace.sol` (new) is a non-custodial peer-to-peer listing contract: a holder who already owns tokens (i.e., by construction, someone past their first purchase) calls `list()` after `setApprovalForAll`-ing the marketplace on the token contract; a buyer calls `buyListing()` and pays native testnet ETH. `PLATFORM_FEE_BPS = 100` (1.00%) of the *token amount* — not the payment — is diverted to `feeRecipient` on every purchase; the seller is still paid in full for everything sold, so the fee comes out of what the buyer receives, not the seller's proceeds. Fee math is `floor(amount × 100 / 10000)`, so trades under 100 tokens round the fee down to 0 — an accepted consequence of an integer token-count fee, documented in the contract rather than worked around. Listings are non-custodial (tokens stay in the seller's wallet until an atomic buy transaction moves them), and `buyListing` follows checks-effects-interactions plus OpenZeppelin's `ReentrancyGuard`. 22 Hardhat tests cover listing/approval/balance checks, the fee split and rounding, full-payment enforcement, listing cancellation, and fee-recipient management — all passing. **Not yet deployed** to Base Sepolia — this is source + tests only, matching how the token contract itself started (§2.10) before a deploy was explicitly requested.
- **Frontend**: Portfolio gained a "Sell" action per holding (only shown when `tokens > 0`, so it's structurally impossible to sell before a first purchase) that mirrors the contract's exact fee formula client-side against the app's existing mocked-portfolio state (this repo's purchase flow has never done real on-chain settlement per-trade — see `asset-detail.component.ts`'s `buy()` — so the sale flow matches that same simulated fidelity rather than introducing real settlement for only one side of the trade). The confirmation modal shows the token quantity, proceeds, and the platform's token fee before the seller confirms, and the i18n copy explaining the rule ships in all 9 supported locales.

**2.12 "Verifiable on-chain" + a real market price — resolved as listing-based resale, not dynamic/algorithmic pricing (29 Aug 2026).** Follow-up request: make the purchase flow "verifiable on-chain" and show a token's price rising as it's bought. Two things got untangled here, one technical and one already-decided-in-this-repo:
- **On-chain-verifiable purchase (technically fine, not yet built)**: `WalletService` today deliberately never requests a signature or transaction (see its own doc comment) — every "buy" in this app, including §2.11's `HumfiverseMarketplace`, is UI-state-only. Making the *primary* purchase a real signed transaction against `HumfiverseCatalogueToken` would be a clean, low-risk extension (a public, priced `buy()` alongside the existing owner-gated `releaseFromPool`) — not done in this pass, flagged for later if wanted.
- **Automatically-rising/algorithmic price (deliberately not built — already decided against in legal-regulatory-notes.md §7.8)**: that note is explicit that a fluctuating price formed by an automated matching mechanism (bonding curve, continuous order book) crosses from "issuer/placement agent" into **MTF/OTF authorization under MiFID II Title II** — a heavier, separate license — which is exactly why §2.3's distribution-vault design and this repo's whole "simulate secondary-market price movement" stance exist. Proposing to apply for MTF authorization directly is a real, multi-month/multi-year regulated-infrastructure undertaking for the business/legal side to pursue with counsel — not something achieved by writing contract code, and orthogonal to this prototype.
- **What shipped instead**: the resale mechanism from §2.11 (`HumfiverseMarketplace`-style listings, mirrored client-side) already lets holders resell — the earlier "tokens aren't resellable" read on the product was inaccurate, just not yet visible in the UI. Added a `SecondaryListing` model + `StoreService.secondaryListings`/`activeListingsFor()`/`lowestAsk()`, seeded with a few mock listings (including one on the already-sold-out `ember-choir` catalogue, at $46 against its $40 primary price — a concrete, honest demonstration of resale price appreciation without any automated pricing mechanism). Portfolio's "Sell" now creates a real listing (seller sets their own price, exactly like `HumfiverseMarketplace.list()`) instead of an instant simulated sale, with a "Your listings" section to cancel one; Asset Detail gained a "Resale marketplace" panel showing active listings, a "Market price" stat tile (the cheapest active listing, i.e. **the lowest active listing method the user confirmed**, not an aggregate/weighted price), and a "Buy" action per listing that applies the same 1% token fee from §2.11. The fee constant was pulled into a shared `core/marketplace-fee.util.ts` so Portfolio and Asset Detail can't drift apart on the formula. i18n shipped in all 9 locales.

- **Frontend**: `webapp` gained `ethers@6` as a real dependency (only place in the Angular app that needs one). `WalletService` — whose own doc comment previously said "no signature or transaction is ever requested" — now has `ensureBaseSepolia()` (switches/adds the network via wallet RPC calls) and `buyOnchain()` (opens a `BrowserProvider`, requests a signature for `buy(tokenId, amount)`, waits for the receipt). This is the *only* path in the app that does this — redeem, resale listings, and preproduction contributions all stay simulated. `asset-detail.component.ts`'s `buy()` now branches: if the asset has a live on-chain price *and* a connected wallet, it submits the real transaction (updating the local mocked portfolio afterward too, matching this repo's established pattern of layering real on-chain state on top of, not replacing, the mock bookkeeping the rest of the app reads — see §2.10's mint endpoint for precedent); otherwise it falls back to the existing simulated purchase, so the app keeps working with no wallet connected. The buy panel shows a live "real purchase" indicator or a connect prompt depending on state, and the success modal shows the real tx hash with an explorer link when applicable. i18n shipped in all 9 locales. `Asset.tokenPrice` (USD, mock display) and the contract's `pricePerToken` (wei, real) are intentionally two different numbers connected only by an illustrative 0.0001 ETH-per-$1 mapping applied at mint time (`onboarding.component.ts`, mirroring `contracts/scripts/catalogues.js`) — there is no real USD/ETH peg here.

## 3. Suggested phased build

1. **Phase 0 — Paper pilot**: one song or small catalogue, manual royalty verification, SPV set up, token issuance and distribution done as a scripted/manual process (not yet a polished product) to prove the legal-to-cash-to-token pipeline end to end with real royalty income, even a small amount. No governance module — if the pilot uses the pre-production variant, milestone escrow is still manually administered.
2. **Phase 1 — Minimum on-chain pilot**: smart contracts for token issuance and claims-based distribution on a testnet, then a single mainnet L2 deployment for the same pilot catalogue, with the royalty-confirmation oracle step still manual/attested. If the pre-production variant is in scope, the milestone-escrow contract ships here — still no governance/voting.
3. **Phase 2 — Marketplace**: multi-catalogue listing, fiat on-ramp, KYC integration, basic secondary transfer (compliant with whichever legal path was chosen). **This is where the advisory governance module (§2.7) ships**, once the SPV-manager role, shortlist-vetting process, and vote-weight caps are properly in place — not before.
4. **Phase 3 — Scale**: decentralize/professionalize the oracle step, consider secondary-market liquidity mechanisms (AMM or order book, subject to what's legally permissible for the token type chosen, and kept architecturally separate from the revenue-distribution vault per §2.3).

## 4. Open technical questions to resolve with the business/legal decisions

- Does the token need on-chain transfer restrictions (security path) or not (collectible path)? This changes the token standard choice directly.
- Will royalty administration be built in-house or outsourced to an existing royalty/PRO administration service in the pilot phase? Outsourcing is almost certainly faster for a first pilot.
- What's the settlement currency for distributions — stablecoin, or fiat off-ramp? Stablecoin is simpler on-chain but adds a conversion step for most retail users; this is worth user-testing rather than assuming.
