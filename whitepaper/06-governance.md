# Governance

## The instinct, and why it needs care

For pre-production campaigns, an obvious idea presents itself: let token holders vote on production choices — which studio, which session musicians — so those decisions are not made through opaque, potentially self-dealing insider relationships. It is a reasonable instinct. It also runs into two real problems that have nothing to do with how the mechanism is marketed.

**Legal exposure.** In November 2024, a US federal court ruled, in litigation concerning the Lido DAO, that participants who vote in a DAO's governance over a real treasury and real decisions can be treated as *general partners in a partnership* under California law — meaning personal, joint-and-several liability for the venture's obligations, not capped at what they invested. The court's reasoning was that real voting over real decisions, backed by a real treasury, is the action of people running a business together, regardless of how decentralized the mechanism is described as being. That is now a live legal risk for any project that gives token holders a binding vote over business decisions, not a hypothetical one — and it argues strongly against a design where "token holders vote and the result executes automatically," at least without a legal wrapper that caps participants' liability or a design where the vote is advisory to a party who retains formal legal responsibility.

**Creative and product risk, independent of the law.** Committee-driven creative decisions are a known failure mode. It is part of why Corite, Kickstarter, and most successful fan-funding platforms deliberately do not give backers control over creative execution, only over whether to fund a project at all. Binding votes on "which singer performs this part" risk slower decisions, watered-down creative choices, and could make the platform actively unattractive to the artists it most needs to attract — the ones who do not want to trade creative control for financing.

## Humfiverse's answer: advisory-first, or not at all yet

Humfiverse's governance module is deliberately **deferred to a later phase**, not part of the current pilot. Phase 0 and Phase 1 (see [Roadmap](07-roadmap.md)) run with no token-holder voting whatsoever, so the harder, more foundational problem — verified royalty data, milestone escrow, and honest distribution — gets proven first, without also debugging a novel governance and liability structure at the same time.

When a governance module does ship, the design intent is deliberately the lighter version: token holders vote on a shortlist the artist or platform has already vetted, so there is no single opaque gatekeeper but also no unbounded crowd decision; the vote is advisory; and a legally accountable party — the entity managing the underlying legal vehicle — retains formal contracting authority and executes accordingly, with the vote and its outcome recorded on-chain for transparency regardless of which way it goes. A vote-weight cap or a quadratic-weighting scheme is treated as a hard launch requirement for this feature, not a later hardening pass — the point of advisory shortlist governance is to avoid concentrating opaque power in a single gatekeeper, and a design with no weight limits just relocates that same concentration into a handful of the largest token holders instead.

## Where "no discretionary power" already exists today, concretely

One piece of this philosophy is not deferred — it is already live in the deployed milestone-escrow contract, and it resolves a tension the legal analysis in the previous chapter surfaces directly. An earlier design gave Humfiverse sole authority to decide whether a production milestone had genuinely been met before releasing escrowed funds. On reflection, that is exactly the kind of active, discretionary management that works against the argument for keeping the underlying legal vehicle outside AIFMD's collective-investment-scheme classification (see [Legal & Regulatory Structure](05-legal-structure.md)).

The contract was redesigned accordingly, and this is not a stated intention — it is the actual, deployed behavior: a milestone releases its tranche only once **both** the campaign's artist and its assigned studio confirm, independently and from their own wallets, that it genuinely happened. There is no function in the contract, for Humfiverse's operator role or anyone else, that can release a tranche on a single party's say-so. If the two sides disagree about whether a milestone was met, funds simply stay locked — there is deliberately no arbitration path and no timeout that resolves the disagreement automatically. Humfiverse's actual role in this design is limited to registering parties and creating campaigns on pre-agreed terms — administrative and custodial acts, not adjudicating whether someone did their job. That narrowing is a genuine, code-level answer to a real legal question, not a governance feature added for its own sake.
