# The Opportunity

## Music royalties are a good fit for tokenization

Most real-world assets are hard to tokenize honestly, because the hard part was never the token — it's proving the asset is real and knowing how it performs. Streaming has already solved the second half of that for music: Spotify, Apple Music, and collecting societies report streams and payouts on a predictable, checkable schedule.

The market has already proven people want this:

| Platform | Structure | Chain | Who can invest |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Royal** | A collectible with revenue rights attached | On-chain | Broadly open |
| **anotherblock** | Tokenized streaming right | Ethereum + Optimism | Broadly open |
| **JKBX** | SEC-registered security, no blockchain | Traditional | US retail, via a formal offering |

Royal has paid real fans real money since 2022, on catalogues from artists like Nas and The Chainsmokers. JKBX proved the same appetite exists even through the slower, fully-regulated route. anotherblock proved an on-chain version specifically in the EU, where Humfiverse also operates. No EU-based, fully compliant competitor doing exactly this at scale had turned up as of this document's research — worth checking again before assuming it stays that way, but a good early sign.

## Who this is for

- **Fans and retail investors** who want small, song-level exposure — not a whole catalogue, not an opaque fund.
- **Independent artists** who want cash now against future royalties, without signing a catalogue over to a label or a buyout fund.
- **Catalogue owners** who want to unlock liquidity from music they already own.

Humfiverse's pilot is built around the first two.

## A risk worth naming directly: AI music and royalty pools

Any platform built on royalty income in 2026 has to deal with a live, unresolved question: does AI-generated music threaten the income being tokenized? The honest answer is more specific than yes or no.

Platforms disagree with each other. TIDAL stopped paying royalties on tracks it classifies as fully AI-generated, starting mid-2026. Spotify still pays normally on AI-generated music and says its real target is fraud, not AI. There's no shared rule yet, and a platform can change its policy after a catalogue is already tokenized.

The bigger, better-documented problem is actually fraud, not AI authorship: industry estimates put streaming fraud at roughly $2 billion a year, and a large share of streams on some AI-generated tracks turned out to be bots, not real listeners. The royalty pools this platform depends on are under real pressure — mostly from fraud and low-quality mass uploads, not from AI music being cut off from payment.

Humfiverse treats this as part of onboarding, not an afterthought: every catalogue discloses what AI tooling (if any) went into it, broken down by vocals, instrumentation, composition, production, and lyrics — the same categories DSPs are converging on. This connects directly to the pre-production model: paying real musicians and a real mix engineer to finish a track doesn't just make it better — it likely makes it *more durable*, moving it away from the kind of track a platform might demonetize later. No DSP has published an exact threshold for how much human input is "enough," so Humfiverse is careful to say this reduces that risk, not that it eliminates it.
