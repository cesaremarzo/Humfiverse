# Legal & Regulatory Structure

> This chapter summarizes research and working decisions, not legal advice, and describes a structure that is **not yet in force** — see [Current Status & Disclaimer](09-status-disclaimer.md). No Humfiverse token has been offered to any investor under any of the structures described below. Any real launch requires jurisdiction-specific confirmation from qualified securities counsel before a single token is sold.

## The one question that determines everything else

Every downstream decision in this chapter follows from a single classification question: **is a Humfiverse token a financial instrument, or not?** The two frameworks that matter most approach this differently, but converge on the same practical answer.

In the US, the Howey test asks whether investors put in money, into a common enterprise, expecting profit from the effort of others. A token that pays holders a share of royalty income collected and managed by a platform satisfies that test on its face — which is why JKBX, one of the closest existing comparables, chose full SEC-qualified securities registration from the start rather than argue its way out of it.

In the EU, MiCA classifies a crypto-asset by what it structurally *is*, not by how it is marketed. MiCA's own text does not cover crypto-assets that already qualify as financial instruments under MiFID II — and a token that distributes real royalty income, functioning economically like a bond or a revenue-participation note, is a strong candidate to fall into that MiFID II carve-out rather than being treated as an "ordinary" MiCA crypto-asset. Humfiverse's working position, pending final confirmation from Luxembourg counsel, is that its tokens are MiFID II financial instruments, and that MiCA/CASP crypto-asset-service-provider licensing is consequently **not** the relevant track. This is not a way of avoiding regulation — securities regulation is, if anything, the heavier of the two tracks — it is a decision about *which* regulator and *which* licensing regime governs, made deliberately rather than by default. The pattern across every serious platform in this space, including Royal's more contested "digital collectible" positioning, is the same: there is no way to tokenize music royalty income and cleanly avoid securities-style regulation once the token pays out real royalty income to holders. The only real choice is which flavor of that compliance to take on, and how much regulatory risk to accept in exchange for speed.

## The legal wrapper: a Luxembourg securitization vehicle

The entity actually holding each catalogue's royalty right is envisioned as a **Luxembourg securitization vehicle** under the Loi du 22 mars 2004 relative à la titrisation (as amended 2022) — a jurisdiction choice made deliberately, and pending final counsel confirmation, for three concrete reasons:

- Luxembourg securitization vehicles support legally segregated **compartments**, potentially allowing one compartment per catalogue rather than a wholly new company per catalogue — a meaningful reduction in recurring structuring cost as the number of listed catalogues grows.
- Luxembourg is the largest EU fund domicile by assets under management, with deep, direct regulatory and market expertise in exactly the securities- and fund-structuring questions this project raises — most importantly the open AIFMD question below.
- Malta, considered and rejected for this specific purpose: Malta's regulatory advantage is built around MiCA/CASP crypto-asset licensing, which is not the relevant track once the MiFID II classification above is settled.

## The collective-investment-scheme question (AIFMD)

Pooling many investors' money into a structure professionally managed toward a return risks being read as an Alternative Investment Fund under the EU's AIFMD, which — if triggered — requires an authorized manager, an independent depositary, and its own capital requirements: a heavy layer on top of MiFID II compliance. Two arguments favor staying outside that classification, neither guaranteed without counsel confirmation: a de minimis registration-only regime exists for managers below scale thresholds, and structuring each catalogue around one specific, pre-identified track rather than a discretionary, diversified portfolio chosen by a manager is a defensible "direct investment" argument rather than a "fund" one.

This is not a purely theoretical distinction for Humfiverse specifically — it directly shaped how the milestone-escrow smart contract works. An earlier design gave Humfiverse sole discretion to decide whether a production milestone had genuinely been met before releasing funds. That is active, discretionary management in the ordinary meaning of the phrase, and it cuts directly against the "not actively managed" argument this section depends on. The resolution, described fully in [Governance](06-governance.md), was to redesign fund release so it requires the artist's and the studio's own on-chain confirmation, with Humfiverse holding no confirmation function of its own at all — a technical design choice made to strengthen a real legal argument, not merely a UX preference.

## The pre-production financing model's additional territory

Financing an *unfinished* track sits in regulatory territory beyond the questions above. The EU's crowdfunding regulation, ECSPR, is a plausible but imperfect fit — it is built around recognized transferable securities or "admitted crowdfunding instruments" and explicitly excludes reward-based crowdfunding, and a royalty-share token sits awkwardly between those categories. Humfiverse's working approach is to structure each pre-production campaign's underlying instrument as a plain, company-law-recognized participative or bond-like instrument, with the token acting as a record of ownership of that instrument rather than a bespoke novel instrument in itself — the structuring choice most likely, though not confirmed, to qualify for ECSPR treatment (capped at €5M raised per project owner per rolling 12 months) rather than falling back to full MiFID II securities treatment for every campaign.

## Secondary trading: a deliberately unbuilt feature, not an oversight

A request that comes up naturally — letting investors buy and sell tokens from each other at a market-discovered, fluctuating price — does not trigger CASP licensing given the MiFID II classification above, but it does trigger something else: authorization as a **Multilateral Trading Facility or Organised Trading Facility** under MiFID II, a separate and generally *heavier* license than being an issuer or placement agent, harmonized EU-wide with no jurisdiction meaningfully easing it. Because of this, Humfiverse's current resale mechanism is deliberately limited to individually priced, non-custodial listings between holders — closer to a bulletin board than a trading venue — with genuine algorithmic price discovery held back as an explicit, licensed, future undertaking rather than something quietly built into a "marketplace" feature.

## Compliance cost, order of magnitude

For a first pilot, Humfiverse's working plan is placement through an already-authorized third party (a bank or existing investment firm) rather than becoming a licensed investment firm itself, with each catalogue's instrument structured to stay under Italy's prospectus-exemption threshold or limited to qualified investors. Rough legal and structuring cost for this path: **€80,000–€250,000**. Humfiverse obtaining its own full investment-firm authorization — needed only for self-placing at scale — carries minimum regulatory capital requirements from roughly €75,000 to €750,000 depending on activity scope, plus legal process cost, totaling roughly **€300,000–€1,000,000+** and a 12–24 month authorization timeline. The ECSPR crowdfunding-provider path for pre-production financing carries its own authorization cost, roughly **€300,000–€800,000**. These figures are order-of-magnitude estimates from research current as of mid-2026, not quotes, and should be reconfirmed with counsel before being relied on for planning.

## Rights and IP, independent of how the token is regulated

Separately from how the token itself is classified, the underlying royalty right has its own, entirely conventional requirements: confirming clean title with no existing liens or conflicting collection agreements before tokenization, structuring the assignment from the rights holder to the legal vehicle so the claim survives the original rights holder's own bankruptcy or change of ownership, and navigating the specific assignment rules of whichever collecting society already administers a given catalogue's rights. In Italy specifically, SIAE is no longer a legal monopoly since the 2017 transposition of the EU Collective Rights Management Directive — independent management entities are a real, operating alternative — but the actual constraint in any given case is the specific mandate terms of whichever society already holds a catalogue's rights, not a platform-wide policy question.

## Investor protection, built into onboarding

Before any purchase, an investor completes an identity and appropriateness check modeled on MiFID II Art. 25(3) — required before selling a complex, non-standard instrument like a royalty-participation note without advice, in an execution-only regime. This records identity fields, a self-declared retail-or-professional classification, and a short appropriateness questionnaire covering prior investment experience with complex or illiquid instruments and an explicit acknowledgment of total-loss risk and the absence of a guaranteed secondary market. A result below threshold produces a clear warning, not an automatic block — consistent with how the real Art. 25(3) regime actually works. This is disclosed as an illustrative implementation of that regime's *shape*, not a certified, counsel-reviewed questionnaire ready for a real regulated launch.
