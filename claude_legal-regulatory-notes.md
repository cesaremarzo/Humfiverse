# Music Royalty Stream Tokenization — Legal & Regulatory Research Notes

*Working draft — v0.1, 26 Aug 2026*

**This is a research summary to orient the project, not legal advice.** Securities and crypto-asset regulation is jurisdiction-specific, fact-specific, and changing quickly (MiCA implementation in Italy is still actively settling as of mid-2026 — see §3). Any real fundraising, token issuance, or investor-facing offering needs sign-off from a securities/fintech lawyer before launch. Treat this doc as the list of questions to bring to that lawyer, not as an answer to them.

## 1. The core classification question

Everything in this project turns on one question: **is the token a security/financial instrument, or not?** The two frameworks that matter most (EU and US) approach this differently, which matters because it determines which regulator, which licensing track, and which investor base applies.

- **US — the Howey test** looks at the transaction: did investors put in money, into a common enterprise, expecting profit, from the effort of others? A token that pays holders a share of royalty income collected and managed by the platform looks, on its face, like it could satisfy all four prongs — which is exactly why JKBX chose to structure its offering as an SEC-qualified security from the start (via a Reg A+-style qualification, selling through a dedicated affiliate entity for each catalogue) rather than try to argue its way out of Howey.
- **EU — MiCA classifies by what the token *is*, structurally**, not primarily by how it's marketed. If a token distributes real royalty income and functions like a bond or equity-like instrument, MiCA's own text is understood to carve this out: MiCA explicitly does not cover crypto-assets that qualify as financial instruments under MiFID II. A royalty-revenue-sharing token is a strong candidate to fall into that MiFID II carve-out rather than being treated as an "ordinary" MiCA crypto-asset — meaning a EU launch is more likely to be a *securities/MiFID II* project wearing blockchain infrastructure than a pure crypto-asset (CASP) project. This is the single most important thing to get a lawyer to confirm early, because it changes the entire regulatory track.

## 2. What existing platforms have chosen (and why it's informative)

- **JKBX (US)**: went all the way to full securities registration/qualification. No blockchain in the loop for the ownership record. This is the "no shortcuts" path — expensive and slow to set up, but gives the clearest legal footing and access to a broad qualified retail base once done.
- **Royal (US)**: sells "Limited Digital Assets," explicitly not marketed as securities, and frames royalty payments as revenue-sharing tied to a collectible rather than an investment contract. This is a real legal position some crypto/music platforms take, but it is *contested terrain* — the SEC has pursued other revenue-share NFT/token projects on Howey grounds elsewhere, so this path carries meaningfully more regulatory risk than JKBX's, in exchange for faster time-to-market and broader access (no accredited-investor gating).
- **anotherblock (EU-based, operating on Ethereum/Optimism)**: closest existing comparable to what an EU-first version of this project might look like. Worth deeper diligence on precisely how they've structured investor eligibility and what disclosures they use, since that's a live example of a MiCA-era EU platform in this exact space.

The pattern: **there is no way to tokenize music royalty income and cleanly avoid securities-style regulation if the token pays out real royalty income to holders.** The only real choice is which flavor of securities compliance to take on, and how much regulatory risk to accept in exchange for speed.

## 3. EU/Italy-specific notes (as of mid-2026)

- MiCA (Regulation (EU) 2023/1114) applies directly across the EU, including Italy, without needing separate implementing legislation for its core provisions.
- In Italy, supervision splits between **CONSOB** (market conduct, investor protection) and **Banca d'Italia** (prudential/financial stability). As of the sources reviewed in mid-2026, Italy had not yet issued a fully MiCA-licensed CASP authorization domestically — platforms have largely used "passporting" from CASP licenses issued in other EU states (Malta and Ireland were named as faster-moving jurisdictions) to serve Italian users, with first Italian home-country licenses potentially not landing until 2027.
- Critically, as noted in §1, if this project's token is legally a financial instrument (likely, given it pays royalty income), **MiCA/CASP licensing may not even be the relevant track** — MiFID II investment-services rules and Italian securities law (and CONSOB's role as market regulator under that framework, not just crypto framework) would likely govern instead. This needs explicit confirmation from counsel, since building for the wrong licensing track wastes significant time and money.
- Italy has separately been rolling out crypto-asset taxation rules through 2026, which will matter for both the platform and token holders' reporting obligations once real royalty distributions start flowing.

## 4. Pre-production financing model (the "fund it before it exists" variant)

The concept doc (§8) sketches a variant where token holders finance production of an unfinished track in exchange for future royalty share, sometimes with a governance vote over production decisions (studio, performers). This isn't just a different product — it likely sits in different, additional regulatory territory on top of everything in §1–3:

- **Crowdfunding regulation (EU: ECSPR, Regulation (EU) 2020/1503) is a plausible additional/alternative framework — but doesn't cleanly fit either.** ECSPR governs EU platforms matching investors with project owners for investment-based crowdfunding (up to €5M raised per project owner per rolling 12 months, above which full prospectus rules under Regulation (EU) 2017/1129 apply). But ECSPR is built around "business crowdfunding" using recognized transferable securities or admitted crowdfunding instruments, and explicitly excludes reward-based crowdfunding — a royalty-share token sits awkwardly between the two, so it likely doesn't get the (comparatively lighter) ECSPR treatment by default and instead falls back to full MiFID II/national securities treatment, or needs a bespoke instrument design that counsel confirms actually qualifies as an ECSPR-admitted instrument. Worth a direct question to counsel rather than an assumption either way.
- **Pooling many investors' money into a common structure, professionally managed toward a return, risks being read as a collective investment scheme (AIFMD).** The EU's Alternative Investment Fund Managers Directive covers vehicles that raise capital from multiple investors to invest per a defined policy for those investors' benefit. An SPV-per-track that pools token-holder money and is "managed" toward a royalty return has at least a surface resemblance to that definition. This doesn't mean it *is* an AIF — scale, structure, and how "collective" vs. "bespoke" each SPV is all matter — but it's a real question to put to counsel before assuming MiFID II/MiCA are the only two frameworks in play, especially once financing (not just secondary tokenization of existing income) is part of the product.
- **Governance/voting rights are a distinct, serious legal exposure of their own — not just a UX feature.** In November 2024, a US federal court ruled (in litigation concerning the Lido DAO) that token holders who participate in binding governance votes over a real treasury and real decisions can be treated as *general partners in a partnership* under California law, carrying personal, joint-and-several liability for the venture's obligations — not capped at their investment. The court's reasoning applies regardless of how "decentralized" the mechanism is marketed to be: real votes over real money and real decisions read as people running a business together. This is now a concrete, current legal risk (not a hypothetical) for the governance mechanism described in the concept doc, and argues for either (a) making votes advisory only, with a licensed/accountable entity retaining formal decision and contracting authority, or (b) wrapping the governance collective itself in a limited-liability structure (e.g. a Wyoming DAO LLC/DUNA-style entity, if a comparable EU wrapper is available) before offering binding votes. This should be resolved with counsel before any voting mechanism is built, not after.
- **Pooled-funds custody needs its own answer, independent of token classification.** PledgeMusic (a non-blockchain fan-funding platform) collapsed in 2019 after commingling new campaigns' funds with old ones once its financial controls fell behind its growth — artists were left owed hundreds of thousands of dollars. Whatever the legal wrapper, raised funds should sit in an escrow (ideally an on-chain, milestone-gated one — see technical architecture doc) rather than a commingled operating account, and this is worth stating as a design commitment in any investor-facing materials, since it's a concrete, checkable answer to a real, precedented failure mode in this exact space.

**4.1 Who actually executes decisions: the "SPV manager" role.** Any advisory-governance design (concept doc §8.3, technical doc §2.7) still needs a real, legally accountable party to sign contracts and control the collection account — a token-holder vote, even unanimous, has no legal force on its own; a company needs a director or equivalent legal representative to act. Realistic candidates: (a) a professional third-party SPV administration firm (common in structured finance — this decouples execution from both the platform and the artist, and such firms typically carry their own professional-indemnity insurance); (b) a management entity set up by the platform itself, appointed as director of each song/catalogue SPV, governed by a written mandate that requires it to consider (but not be bound by) token-holder advisory votes, with deviations documented and disclosed; (c) the artist as director of their own SPV, simplest but concentrates both creative and execution power back in one party, cutting against the anti-opacity goal; or (d) a small board combining more than one of the above. Whichever is chosen, this is a normal corporate-governance/company-law question (director duties, conflicts of interest, liability insurance — for an Italian SPV, obligations under the *codice civile* for *amministratori*), not a blockchain question, and needs local corporate counsel, separate from the securities-classification counsel in §1.

## 5. Rights & IP considerations (distinct from securities law)

Independent of how the token itself is regulated, the underlying royalty right has its own legal requirements:
- Confirming clean title to the royalty stream (no existing liens, prior assignments, or conflicting collection agreements) before it's tokenized — this is standard music-industry diligence, not blockchain-specific, but it's the step every platform in this space treats as foundational.
- Structuring the assignment/license from the rights holder to the SPV correctly so that the SPV's claim (and therefore the token holders' claim) is legally enforceable and survives the rights holder's own bankruptcy or change of ownership (a "true sale" or equivalent structure, the same issue royalty-buyout funds like Hipgnosis have had to solve).
- Collection society/PRO rules (e.g., SIAE in Italy) around assignment of royalty collection rights can be restrictive and vary by territory — worth checking early whether SIAE (or the relevant PRO for the first pilot catalogue's territory) permits the specific assignment structure the SPV needs.

## 6. Questions to bring to counsel before any real launch

1. Given the payout structure described in the concept doc, does counsel agree this token is a financial instrument under MiFID II (EU) / a security under Howey (US), or is there a structuring approach that changes that analysis?
2. Which jurisdiction should the first pilot launch in, and does that change based on where the pilot artist/catalogue and target investors are located?
3. What's the minimum viable compliance structure for a *pilot* (e.g., accredited/qualified investors only, or a specific EU private-placement exemption) versus what's needed for a full public retail launch?
4. What are SIAE's (or the relevant PRO's) specific rules on assigning royalty collection rights to a third-party SPV?
5. What tax treatment applies to royalty distributions received by token holders, and what reporting obligations does that create for the platform?
6. If pursuing the pre-production financing model (§4): does the SPV-per-track structure risk classification as an AIF under AIFMD, and does the royalty-share instrument have any realistic path to ECSPR treatment, or is full MiFID II/national-securities treatment the only option?
7. If pursuing token-holder governance in any binding form: what legal wrapper (if any) would shield participating token holders from partnership-style liability, given the Lido DAO precedent, and does an EU-law equivalent to that protection currently exist?
